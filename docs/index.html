Bal's Sound Garden

A cozy music-making adventure and shop-management game built in Python without a conventional game engine. The project combines an endless exploration world, a procedural audio system, a full in-game sequencer, and a persistent story simulation.

What the player does

The game connects exploration, composition and shop management

Each major system feeds the next one instead of existing as a separate menu or minigame.

You play as Bal, a well-known cat music maker who arrives on a new island and opens its music shop. Animals visit with requests for lullabies, celebrations, travel music, night pieces, rhythms, sound seeds and location-based commissions. Bal can accept a request or decline it. Accepted requests must be completed before the deadline.

Exploration supplies the musical material. Notes, bass tones, chords, drums, rhythms, melodic phrases, ambience and effects appear throughout the world as collectible components. A collected component is not a score token or an abstract unlock. It is an actual playable sound that becomes available in the Music Maker and the Soundbook.

The resulting songs can be performed for clients, saved as projects, exported as WAV files, or placed in the world through gramophones. Individual sounds can also be planted as sprouts. Sprouts play on the garden beat when Bal or an animal crosses their ring, which allows the environment itself to perform simple arrangements.

Story mode adds the shop clock, client appointments, deadlines, coins, reputation, a travelling merchant, decorations, visible upgrades and three animal helpers. Creative mode keeps the original free-play world and music sandbox separate, with its own collection, songs and world state.

  • ExploreWalk, run, jump, swim, cross bridges, collect sounds and find different musical regions.
  • ComposeArrange the collected material on a multitrack step grid, connect tiles into phrases, apply effects and play the result.
  • Run the shopRead structured briefs, manage time and deadlines, deliver music, earn coins and build the business.
  • Change the worldPlant sound sprouts, place looping gramophones, install decorations and complete field commissions at persistent locations.

World and rendering

The 2.5D world is drawn and projected by the game

The visual system is a custom Pygame renderer rather than a level made in a commercial engine.

World-space coordinates and perspective

The playable world is stored in horizontal X and depth Z coordinates. A focal-length perspective calculation converts depth into screen scale, moves distant objects toward the meadow horizon, and offsets their horizontal position relative to the camera. This gives the scene depth while keeping movement and collision on a simple ground plane.

Visible props, water structures, animals, the player and world objects are gathered into one depth list and sorted by their projected ground position. That shared ordering is what lets Bal pass naturally in front of or behind trees, animals, bridges, boats, buildings and planted objects.

Runtime-drawn art

Most of the world art is created at runtime with Pygame shapes. Trees, flowers, stones, grass, buildings, water details, the cat and the animal roster are assembled from polygons, ellipses, circles and lines. This makes animation and perspective scaling part of the same drawing system instead of requiring a large set of pre-rendered sprites for every pose and size.

The world first renders to a low-resolution logical canvas based on a 384 × 216 composition. It is then presented with a fixed integer scale to preserve hard pixel edges. Fullscreen does not simply enlarge the same image. It expands the logical canvas around the original composition, so a larger display reveals more of the world while Bal and the interface keep the same physical size.

Stable motion and controlled detail

The horizontal camera origin is quantized to the logical pixel grid. Without this step, distant objects would cross different rounding boundaries during small camera movements and the background would appear to shimmer. Snapping the world movement to shared pixel increments keeps the far field stable while forward and backward depth movement remains continuous.

Rendering work is limited by depth, scale and screen bounds. Small decorations disappear before they become visual noise, distant objects blend toward the atmospheric haze, and close grass uses a separate denser pass. The result keeps nearby areas detailed without processing the entire endless world every frame.

Painted atmosphere

Mountains, mist, ground color, water haze and distant props use coordinated fading so depth remains readable without a 3D engine.

Day and night

Cached sky stages cover dawn, morning, noon, golden hour, sunset, dusk and night, with a moving sun, moon, stars and seeded auroras.

Water geometry

Coastlines, rivers, ponds and lakes are generated separately from ordinary scenery and provide depth-aware swimming, crossings, boats and shoreline rules.

Music as light

Sprouts and gramophones create warm pools of light after dark, allowing sound objects to affect both audio and world presentation.

Endless island

The same seed rebuilds the same places

The world can extend indefinitely without saving a complete map.

A new profile receives one persistent world seed. The seed is stored with the player's world state and is reused on later launches. World space is divided into fixed-size cells, and each cell derives its own stable random seed from the world seed and its coordinates.

Different parts of a cell use separate deterministic streams. Gameplay scenery, decorative dressing and close ground cover do not consume the same random sequence. This means adding a visual detail to one layer does not silently move the trees, water or collectibles generated by another layer.

Only the cells near the camera are generated and kept in memory. Scenery farther away can be removed from the cache because it can be reconstructed exactly from the same coordinate hash when the player returns. Water layout, animal placement and musical pickups follow the same deterministic approach.

Player-made changes are stored separately from generated scenery. The save data records the seed and the things that cannot be reconstructed: collected components, planted sprouts, gramophones, decorations, finished work, machine state, story progress and other persistent actions. This keeps the save compact while allowing the island to remain consistent.

Animal system

Twenty species use the same procedural art and animation pipeline

The animal visitors and wildlife are generated from the game's drawing language rather than imported character sheets.

The active roster contains rabbits, dogs, foxes, raccoons, squirrels, frogs, turtles, pigs, goats, chickens, ducks, birds, butterflies, deer, sheep, cows, horses, owls, hedgehogs and tanuki. Each species defines its size, habitat, temperament, speed and spawn weighting, while the renderer supplies its proportions, markings and pose.

Animation is calculated from body-part positions. Walking changes leg angles, body height, head movement and forward lean. Idle animals breathe, blink and move their ears. Hoppers tuck their legs, sleeping animals settle into lower poses, and friendly animals can react with playful expressions and effects.

The behavior layer uses readable states such as wandering, resting, sleeping, fleeing, approaching and playing. Story clients add another state machine on top of the same animal actors: travelling to the shop, queueing, waiting, leaving for an appointment, returning, listening to a performance, reacting and walking away.

Sound catalog and synthesis

The collected items are real audio building blocks

The catalog, infinite generator and synthesizer are designed as one system.

The finite catalog

The game contains 2,946 authored components before infinite generation is considered. This includes the original hand-built set and a 2,668-entry Sound Atlas. Ten starter components are available immediately, while the rest are distributed by family, region and rarity.

The Sound Atlas expands the palette across 47 melodic or tonal voices, 44 drum definitions, 28 ambience sources and 17 effect types. Components are assigned to the Meadow, Rhythm Path, Riverbank, Grove of Chords, Deepwood, Animal Corners, Hidden Clearings or the separate reward collection. Common material appears closer to the beginning, while rarer and more unusual sounds are placed deeper into exploration.

ComponentDNA v2

Unexplored areas can continue producing new sounds after the finite catalog is exhausted. Generated components use compact versioned identifiers such as proc2.<seed>. The identifier is enough to reconstruct the component definition and waveform, so procedural discoveries do not require full audio files in the save.

BLAKE2s hashing creates separate named random streams for identity, harmony, rhythm, timbre, naming, drum-kit design, ambience and effects. A change to the rhythm generator therefore does not unexpectedly rename a component or change its key. Older proc.<seed> components keep their original generator so existing saves continue to reproduce the same sounds.

The generator covers ten musical families and twelve style grammars, including lullaby, forest folk, nocturne, ambient, cinematic, garden jazz, clockwork, electronic, pixel garden, world garden, glitch bloom and animal chorus. It chooses from fifteen scales and modes, then builds motifs, chord voicings, progressions, drum kits, grooves, layered soundscapes and controlled effect chains. Rarity changes structural complexity rather than simply marking a sound as better.

NumPy and SciPy audio

Audio is synthesized as 44.1 kHz NumPy floating-point buffers. The engine combines sine, triangle, saw and square oscillators with noise, harmonics, detuning, frequency modulation and ADSR envelopes. Instrument families have different synthesis recipes for keys, bells, plucks, winds, pads, strings, basses, animal voices and percussion.

SciPy supplies Butterworth filtering and FFT-based convolution. Filters shape low and high frequencies, while convolution creates the reverb path. The rest of the effect system includes delay, tremolo, chorus, phasing, drive, ring modulation, vibrato, gating, compression, resonators, bit reduction and generated multi-stage chains.

Music Maker

The sequencer uses the same audio path for editing, playback and export

The composition interface is not a mock screen. It builds the projects used by the rest of the game.

A project begins at 100 BPM with eight bars and a set of melody, chord, drum and loop tracks. The timeline uses eight eighth-note steps per bar. Players can change the tempo, extend the song from two to thirty-two bars, add tracks for any supported family, rename them, duplicate them, mute them, adjust volume and assign collected track effects.

Every placed tile stores a component identifier and a grid position. Its audible length is derived from the component itself: a drum hit occupies a short event, while a rhythm, loop or ambience block can cover several steps or bars. Project files remain small because they store arrangement data rather than rendered audio.

Connected phrases

The Connect tool lets several tiles on one track behave as one phrase. Sustaining voices receive one attack, a continuous body and one release. Chords use voice-leading lanes, ringing instruments overlap or extend naturally, drums keep their individual hits, ambience sections crossfade, and imported waveform material can use a sampler-style sustain. Ordinary unconnected tiles continue through the original render path.

Phrase rendering is deterministic and cached with bounded memory. Editing one phrase does not force every other phrase to be regenerated, and missing or damaged members fall back safely instead of making the whole song unusable.

Playback and WAV export

The Music Maker, world performances, gramophones and file exporter all use the same project mixer. That creates a clear “what you hear is what you export” boundary. Repeated sounds and effects use bounded LRU caches, while unique clips are mixed incrementally so dense projects do not require every intermediate buffer to remain in memory.

Export produces a standard 44.1 kHz, 16-bit stereo PCM WAV file. Rendering runs in a background worker from a snapshot of the current project, so the window remains responsive and later edits cannot change an export already in progress. The completed file is written to a temporary sibling and then published with an atomic replacement, which prevents an interrupted export from damaging an older file with the same name.

Story simulation

The shop continues operating while Bal is composing

Time, requests and client states are part of the same simulation as exploration.

A live shop day

A full Story day lasts twenty real minutes by default. The shop opens at 09:00 and closes at 18:00. The clock continues while Bal explores and while the Music Maker is open, but pauses when the game itself is paused. Walk-in clients have patience, accepted work has deadlines, and scheduled visitors can return later in the day or on a later date.

Clients are not fixed menu entries. They walk into the world, form a queue, wait, speak, leave temporarily, return for appointments, listen to performances, react to the result and physically depart. Completed clients can return one to four days later with dialogue that refers to the previous job.

Deterministic request generation

The request system combines more than fifty authored occasion types with species preferences, habitat, day, visit number, destination, recipient, difficulty, tempo range, required musical families, mood tags, energy, patience and delivery style. The same world seed, day, request serial, species and visit number reproduce the same structured brief, which keeps saving and testing reliable.

Requests can ask for a song at the counter, a collected sound seed, a sprout planted at a marked location, or a gramophone placed in the field. Active field jobs remain in the save and display a direction and distance marker until the object is delivered at the real destination.

Explainable evaluation

Song results are based on arrangement data rather than hidden audio classification. The game checks required families, dominant mood tags, tempo, energy, effort and cohesion. Missing a required family caps the result. Reusing the same arrangement reduces its freshness even when the song is renamed, because the system hashes the musical contents rather than the title.

Sound-seed requests compare the selected component's type, tags, home region and rarity. Result screens can therefore explain why a delivery matched or missed the brief instead of returning an unexplained score.

Coins and visible growth

Poko's travelling cart sells unowned sounds, twenty placeable decorations, five shop upgrades and helper contracts. Decorations remain in storage until Bal places them on clear ground. Lanterns and glowing jars emit light at night, while the waiting bench gives visitors a small patience benefit.

The upgrades appear physically around the shop: a request board, listening corner, sound cabinet, courier shelf and larger counter. Juniper the fox searches selected regions for sounds, Clover the rabbit records new offers while Bal is away, and Bramble the dog carries prepared ordinary field deliveries. They organize work but never compose music for the player.

Persistence and release work

The project includes the reliability work needed around the game itself

Save handling, export safety, packaging and regression testing were treated as part of the finished project.

Separate profiles and portable storage

Story and Creative mode keep separate collections, worlds and song projects. Settings and shared resources remain outside the profile switch. Source builds can use local folders, while packaged builds redirect writable data to the operating system's per-user application-data location instead of writing beside an executable or inside a macOS application bundle.

Older local saves can be copied forward without overwriting newer files. Exported WAV files are treated as user documents and are not deleted by Reset Progress.

Transactional saves

Central JSON saves are serialized and validated before the final file is replaced. The game writes a unique temporary file in the destination directory, flushes it, reopens it for validation, and then commits it with an atomic replace. When an operation fails, the old file remains intact and the in-memory change is rolled back where necessary.

Malformed song projects are preserved on disk, skipped safely and reported with a non-blocking warning. Critical persistence, loading, audio and delivery failures are recorded in a bounded rotating log rather than being silently ignored.

Packaging and tests

The package metadata targets Python 3.8 or newer and declares Pygame, NumPy and SciPy as its runtime dependencies. The repository includes wheel configuration, a PyInstaller specification and a clean release-staging tool that excludes player saves, exports, logs, caches, tests and development notes from public archives.

The release test suite covers atomic persistence, failed overwrites, malformed projects, profile storage, WAV validity, deterministic synthesis, procedural reconstruction, catalog coverage, world pickup placement, story deadlines, courier transactions, client names, UI regressions and package contents. The focused release-hardening, packaging, ComponentDNA and Sound Atlas suites pass in headless Pygame.

The published release is available for Windows, Linux, Intel-based Macs and Apple silicon Macs. The platform builds were packaged and validated through GitHub Actions.
